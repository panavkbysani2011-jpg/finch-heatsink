01

The thermal model

A thin metal plate is discretised onto a square grid. Each cell is either metal or air. Heat is transported laterally through the metal by conduction and lost from both plate faces by convection and radiation. At steady state the energy balance for any metal cell is:

Σ Gcond (Tn − T) − 2(heff + hrad) Δx² (T − T∞) + Q = 0

This produces one linear equation per metal cell, solved simultaneously as a sparse system. The conductance between adjacent cells is Gcond = k·tz, which is independent of grid spacing because the increase in face area exactly cancels the increase in centre distance.

Convection with channel starvation

Treating the convection coefficient as uniform across the surface is a common simplification, but it overstates the performance of densely packed geometry. Air flowing through a narrow channel between two heated walls approaches the wall temperature and ceases to remove heat effectively. Equating the rate at which the walls deliver heat to the rate at which the flow can carry it away defines a critical gap width:

sc = 2hL / (ρ u cp)

This corresponds to a channel Number of Transfer Units of unity. The effective coefficient is then blended between the starved and isolated-plate limits:

heff(s) = hiso (1 − e−s/sc)

The formulation follows the composite approach of Bar-Cohen and Rohsenow for parallel-plate channels. At s = 0 the coefficient vanishes, representing fully enclosed metal in stagnant air; at large s it recovers the isolated-plate value. The single length scale is derived from an energy balance rather than assigned. For the reference configuration sc = 4.645 mm.

Local gap width is obtained from a Euclidean distance transform of the air region, taking the widest channel adjacent to each metal cell.

Radiation

Surface radiation is included via the Stefan-Boltzmann law, linearised into an equivalent coefficient so that it can enter the same linear system:

hrad = ε σ (T² + T∞²)(T + T∞)

Because this depends on temperature, the system is non-linear and is iterated to convergence. For anodised aluminium (ε ≈ 0.85) at 200 °C the radiative contribution is of the order of 10 W/m²K, comparable to natural convection, and is therefore not negligible.

Transient response

A time-dependent solver integrates the same energy balance with a thermal capacity term, permitting warm-up behaviour and thermal mass to be examined:

ρcp tz Δx² dT/dt = Σ Gcond(Tn − T) − 2htotΔx²(T − T∞) + Q

03

Designing the objective function

In an evolutionary search the objective function fully determines the outcome. The algorithm maximises precisely the quantity it is given, including any unintended interpretation of that quantity. Four successive formulations were evaluated before one proved robust.

FormulationPeakMeanObserved behaviour
Minimise mean temperature288.9 237.0Extended cool material distant from the source to reduce the average, producing the highest peak temperature of all formulations
Minimise peak temperature283.9 271.4Expanded without limit in the absence of a material constraint
Peak temperature with material budget283.9 271.4Produced single-cell features at the resolution limit, where the discretisation is least reliable
Peak temperature, budget and width penalty284.9 261.8No exploitable behaviour identified

The first formulation achieved the lowest mean temperature and the highest peak temperature simultaneously. This demonstrates that the choice of objective, rather than the optimisation algorithm, determines whether a search produces a useful design.

The final formulation, used for all reported results, is:

score = −[ Tpeak + 0.15 · fthin · (Tpeak − T∞) ]

subject to a hard material budget and a connectivity requirement, where fthin is the fraction of cells with fewer than two orthogonal metal neighbours.

04

Establishing a fair control group

Any claimed improvement is meaningful only relative to a properly constructed reference design. Three requirements were identified as necessary for a valid comparison, each established through testing.

REQUIREMENT ONE

Equal material allocation

Both designs must be permitted an identical number of metal cells. The conventional design is generated across a range of fin counts and thicknesses, then trimmed from its outermost extremities until it occupies exactly the available budget. Without this step the reference design occupied only 29% of the permitted material while the optimised design used the full allocation, and the resulting comparison substantially overstated the benefit of optimisation.

REQUIREMENT TWO

Correct hub placement

Radial fin arrays are constructed about a hub. The hub must be located at the centroid of the heat source rather than the centre of the domain, since a heat sink is physically mounted on the component it cools. For an edge-mounted source the two positions differ by 14.8 cells, and the incorrect placement penalises the reference design severely.

REQUIREMENT THREE

Optimised reference configuration

The reference is selected as the best performing of twenty-four conventional configurations under the identical thermal model, rather than an arbitrary single design. The optimised geometry is therefore compared against a well-constructed conventional design, not a weak one.

Control group construction proved to be the dominant source of error in this investigation. In every case where an unusually large improvement was observed, the cause was traced to an inadequately specified reference design rather than to the optimisation method.

05

When geometric optimisation is worthwhile

The governing dimensionless parameter is L·m, the ratio of the sink half-width to the characteristic conduction length 1/m, where m = √(2h / k tz). It expresses how far heat can travel through the metal relative to the size of the component.

ConfigurationConduction lengthReferenceOptimised Improvement
Aluminium 1.0 mm, natural convection101 mm 212.1 °C203.9 °C 4.6%
Aluminium 1.0 mm, forced convection41 mm 143.2 °C122.0 °C 19.5%
Aluminium 0.3 mm, forced convection23 mm 157.8 °C141.5 °C 13.3%
Steel 0.5 mm, forced convection14 mm 185.1 °C166.5 °C 12.4%
Steel 0.3 mm, high-velocity flow8 mm 185.1 °C170.2 °C 10.0%

Improvement is not monotonic in L·m. Two competing effects produce an intermediate optimum:

  • At low L·m conduction distributes heat readily across the entire structure, so the temperature field is nearly uniform and material placement has limited influence.
  • At high L·m heat cannot reach the periphery regardless of geometry, so no arrangement of material can exploit the outer region.

Automated geometric optimisation yields the greatest benefit in the intermediate regime, where conduction reaches a useful fraction of the domain but not all of it. Improvements of 3 to 20 percent were obtained across the range examined, with results reproducible to within 0.1 percentage points across independent random seeds.

06

Quality diversity search

A second search strategy was implemented to determine whether maintaining a library of structurally distinct solutions would outperform conventional evolutionary search. The MAP-Elites algorithm of Mouret and Clune partitions a space of shape descriptors into discrete bins and retains the best solution encountered within each, so that unconventional but potentially useful geometries are never discarded.

Two descriptors were used: elongation, defined as the ratio of principal axes of the material distribution, and reach, the mean distance of material from the heat source. Descriptor ranges were determined empirically from sampled designs rather than assigned, since arbitrary normalisation caused all designs to occupy a single bin.

Method, 2400 evaluations eachMean peak temperature Improvement over reference
Conventional fin reference229.90 °C n/a
Evolutionary search213.83 °C 8.2%
Quality diversity search222.95 °C 3.6%

Conventional evolutionary search produced superior single solutions in all four trials. The explanation is a straightforward allocation of effort: the evolutionary method concentrated all 2400 evaluations on refining a single lineage, whereas the quality diversity method distributed the same budget across 134 occupied bins, approximately 17 evaluations each.

The literature reports that quality diversity methods frequently also improve the single best solution found. That outcome was not observed for this problem. The method's value here is instead the library of viable alternative geometries it produces, which is useful when manufacturing or packaging constraints eliminate the single optimum.

A related structural observation emerged from this work. Forty independent restarts with large mutation rates occupied only nine of one hundred bins. At 14% areal fill with a connectivity requirement about a central source, the space of realisable geometries is intrinsically limited: connected structures at this density are necessarily approximately isotropic.

07

Future scope

The following extensions would increase the fidelity and applicability of the investigation. They are ordered by expected value relative to effort.

PRIORITY ONE

Experimental validation

Manufacture two geometries, one conventional and one optimised, by laser cutting or additive manufacturing. Apply a resistive heating element of known power and instrument each with thermocouples. Direct measurement would convert the present simulation results from a modelling prediction into an empirical finding, and would independently test the channel starvation formulation.

PRIORITY TWO

Three-dimensional extension

The present model is two-dimensional. Extending to three dimensions would capture out-of-plane conduction and allow genuine fin height to be optimised alongside plan geometry, at the cost of substantially greater computational expense.

PRIORITY THREE

Computational fluid dynamics coupling

The channel starvation model captures the reduction in convective performance within enclosed geometry using a derived length scale, but does not resolve flow direction, boundary layer development or turbulence. Coupling to a finite-volume flow solver would remove this approximation, and would additionally permit optimisation against pressure drop as a second objective.

PRIORITY FOUR

Manufacturing constraints

Manufacturability is presently expressed as a penalty term weighted at 0.15 rather than as a hard constraint. Formulating explicit minimum feature size, draft angle and tool access constraints would ensure that every generated geometry is directly producible by a specified process.

PRIORITY FIVE

Multi-objective formulation

The current objective minimises peak temperature alone. Practical designs balance thermal performance against mass, material cost, pressure drop and acoustic noise. A Pareto formulation would expose the trade-offs between these quantities rather than returning a single solution.

Scope of present conclusions

Results are obtained from a two-dimensional steady-state conduction model with a derived channel-starvation convection treatment and linearised surface radiation. The model is validated against analytical solutions to within 0.001% and independently reimplemented with agreement to 0.0002 °C. Conclusions describe geometries that are superior within this modelling framework and constitute a hypothesis regarding physical performance, pending the experimental validation described above.
