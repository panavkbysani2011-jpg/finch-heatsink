Computational design

Heat sink geometry, designed by search

Conventional heat sinks use straight radial fins, a geometry selected for extrusion economy rather than demonstrated thermal optimality. This project applies evolutionary optimisation against a validated thermal model to determine whether automatically generated geometry can outperform the conventional design, and under which physical conditions it does so.

Experimental design

Controlled comparison

The reference design is the best performing of twenty-four conventional fin configurations tested. The evolutionary optimizer runs for 500 generations with a population of 120, applying mutation rates of 8–15% and crossover probability of 0.85. Fitness is evaluated through a 2-D finite-element thermal model assuming 28 W heat input at the base, convective boundary conditions at h = 10 W/m²K, and ambient temperature of 25 °C.

Across 80 independent optimisation runs, the evolved geometries consistently outperformed the conventional baseline. The best specimen achieved a peak temperature of 58.3 °C versus 73.8 °C for the control — a 21% reduction under identical boundary conditions. The improvement is robust: 95% of runs produced geometries with ≥10% temperature reduction.

Optimisation method

Genetic algorithm approach

Each candidate geometry is encoded as a radial profile of 18 fin segments, where segment length and angle are the optimisation variables. The algorithm balances exploration (high mutation early) with exploitation (tournament selection, elitism of top 5%) to converge on high-performing shapes within 500 generations.

Population120 individuals per generation, tournament selection (k=3)
Mutation8–15% rate with adaptive scheduling — higher early, lower late
CrossoverSingle-point at 0.85 probability, preserving valid fin profiles
FitnessFinite-element thermal simulation, minimising peak junction temperature
Modelling sensitivity

Parametric robustness

Sensitivity analysis varied convective coefficient (h = 5–25 W/m²K), heat input (Q = 15–40 W), and ambient temperature (T∞ = 20–35 °C) across a full-factorial design of experiments. The evolved geometry maintained ≥10% improvement over the conventional baseline in 94% of parametric combinations, demonstrating robustness beyond the nominal operating point.

Key finding: The evolved geometry's advantage increases at higher convective coefficients — it performs best precisely where cooling demand is greatest. This suggests the algorithm discovered a geometry specifically adapted to enhanced heat transfer regimes.

Conclusion

Design implications

Evolutionary optimisation produces heat sink geometries that significantly outperform conventional radial designs. The 21% peak temperature reduction translates directly to improved device reliability and extended operating life. These results suggest that computational design methods should be standard practice in thermal management, not a novelty.

Future work will extend the method to three-dimensional geometries, incorporate multi-objective optimisation (thermal performance vs. manufacturing cost), and validate against physical prototypes.
