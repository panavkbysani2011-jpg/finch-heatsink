Computational design

Heat sink geometry, designed by search

Conventional heat sinks use straight radial fins, a geometry selected for extrusion economy rather than demonstrated thermal optimality. This project applies evolutionary optimisation against a validated thermal model to determine whether automatically generated geometry can outperform the conventional design, and under which physical conditions it does so.

Method

Method

The method is population-based stochastic search, not machine learning. No model is trained and no prior knowledge of heat sink geometry is encoded. Candidate designs are evaluated against the thermal model and selected on measured performance alone.

01 · GENERATE

Generate

A population of twenty-four candidate geometries is grown outward from the heat source, guaranteeing structural connectivity.

02 · SIMULATE

Evaluate

Each candidate is solved for its steady-state temperature field: conduction, channel-limited convection and surface radiation, one equation per cell.

03 · SELECT AND BREED

Select and recombine

The highest performing candidates are retained, recombined and mutated to form the subsequent generation. The cycle repeats until convergence.

Improvement arises from selection pressure rather than from any learned representation. Poorly performing geometries are eliminated, so the surviving population improves across generations.

The approach has established engineering precedent. NASA applied evolutionary optimisation to the X-band antenna for the Space Technology 5 mission in 2006; the resulting geometry outperformed the contractor-designed alternative and became the first computer-evolved hardware flown in space.

Modelling sensitivity

Convective assumptions determine the result

Computing surface heat loss requires an assumption about the convection coefficient. The standard simplification treats it as uniform across the geometry. This overstates performance for densely packed material, since air within a narrow channel approaches the wall temperature and ceases to remove heat. A channel-starvation formulation was therefore developed and the analysis repeated.

Convective treatmentImprovement
Uniform coefficient, optimised geometry +20.2%
Same geometry, evaluated with channel starvation -6.0%
Re-optimised under channel starvation +9.1%

Under the channel-starvation model the optimiser abandons compact geometry and produces extended branches separated by open channels, independently arriving at the structural principle underlying conventional fin design before improving upon its arrangement.

Conventional fin geometry is well matched to the physical problem: every surface is exposed to open flow on both sides. The result demonstrates that a modelling assumption which appears conservative can materially alter an optimisation outcome, and that convergence of an automated method towards an established design is evidence of model validity.

Results
