Technical notes

Reading the display

A heat sink, seen from above. Inside an LED bulb or a charger there is a chip that gets hot, and a lump of metal whose only job is to move that heat into the air.

The white outline is the chip. Red is hot, dark blue is cold. Dark blue metal is a problem: heat never reached it, so it is dead weight.

The two numbers

The big temperature is the hottest point in that design. Lower is better, because the hottest point is what kills a chip.

The wasted percentage is how much of the metal is sitting near room temperature. Straight fins often waste 40 to 77 percent. A grown design usually wastes almost none.

Convective model

The standard simplification treats the convection coefficient as uniform. This overstates performance for enclosed material, where air approaches the wall temperature and ceases to remove heat.

Under a uniform coefficient the optimiser produces compact geometry and reports 20.2%. Evaluating that same geometry with channel starvation gives 6.0% worse than the conventional design. Re-optimising under the correct model produces extended branches separated by open channels and a genuine 9.1% improvement.

Conventional fin geometry is well matched to the physics: every surface has open flow on both sides.

Effect of source position

Radial geometry is implicitly optimised for a centrally located source. Relocating the source alters the problem, though the effect is moderate once the hub is correctly positioned.

The fin hub is located at the centroid of the heat source in every configuration, consistent with physical mounting practice. Improvement remains between 2 and 11 percent across all source positions examined.

What the materials mean

Three things change together: the metal, its thickness, and the fan. They combine into one number, how far heat can travel before leaking into the air. The sink is 33 mm across.

Thick aluminium, no fan101 mm4.6%
Thick aluminium, fan41 mm19.5%
Thin aluminium, fan23 mm13.3%
Thick steel, fan14 mm12.4%
Thin steel, strong fan8 mm10.0%

Aluminium conducts approximately four times better than steel, and increased thickness raises conductance proportionally. Higher flow velocity increases surface heat transfer but shortens the conduction length, since heat is rejected before travelling far.

Improvement is not monotonic. Where conduction reaches the entire structure the temperature field is nearly uniform and geometry has limited influence; where conduction reaches only a small fraction, no arrangement can exploit the periphery. The greatest benefit occurs in the intermediate regime.

Why does it stop?

Because it has finished. About 95% of the improvement arrives in the first fifty rounds. After seventy rounds with no progress it stops itself. Change the chip position or the material instead of running longer.
